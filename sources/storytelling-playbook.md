# The Storytelling Playbook: Kallaway × Caleb Ralston × Sam Gaudet × Aayush Swamy

Aryan Thakkar · last updated 16 Oct 2026

## Start here: the 12 laws all three agree on

All three teach the same core idea in different words: a great story manages curiosity second by second and builds trust story by story. Kallaway explains the brain science of attention. Caleb Ralston explains how stories turn into a world people want to live in. Sam Gaudet explains how to ship it fast with just a phone.

This playbook comes from 30+ of their videos published between Oct 2025 and Oct 2026, read from full transcripts. Read the 12 laws first, then each creator's section, then the debates, the master template and your practice system.

## 1. Kallaway: the attention scientist

Kallaway (2M followers; Kallaway Marketing on YouTube and Instagram) treats a story as engineered attention: every line either opens a question or pays one off. His four core frameworks are Story Loops, the Dopamine Ladder, the Dopamine Addiction Loop and the 6 Story Locks.

### 1.1 The 6 core techniques

1. **The dance.** Alternate context and conflict. Conflict opens a loop; context closes it. Use the South Park rule (Trey Parker and Matt Stone): between story beats put "but" or "therefore", never "and then". His Stanley Cup video packs four of these turns into 30 seconds.
2. **Rhythm.** Vary sentence length (Gary Provost's famous "this sentence has five words" passage). Write each sentence on its own line; the right edge of the script should look jagged.
3. **Tone.** Talk with people, not at them (Emma Chamberlain, Casey Neistat, Steve Jobs' 2007 iPhone keynote). Write and film for one close friend.
4. **Direction.** Write the last line first; he calls it the "last dab", a line memorable enough to repeat. In short form the last line also sets up the loop back to the first. Write first and last lines, then fill the middle.
5. **Story lenses.** Your unique angle on a topic everyone covers. When Taylor Swift went to the Super Bowl, most creators covered her outfit; he covered her business impact on the NFL and got about 1M views.
6. **The hook.** The first line is punchy and names the plot (never "wait till you see this"). Visual hooks beat audio-only ones: show while you tell.

### 1.2 Story Loops: the building block of every story

Zoom into any story arc and you find a chain of micro-loops. Each loop is **context** (opens it) then **reveal** (closes it), and the next loop opens as the last one closes.

- **Context must be clear.** Confused viewers leave; curious viewers start predicting.
- **Five possible reveals:** worse than expected (they leave), as expected (attention fades), better than expected (they stay), unexpected but confusing (they leave), unexpected but intriguing (they stay). Aim for better-than-expected or intriguing.
- **His 4-step scripting process:**
  1. List every fact or take you have.
  2. Pick the reveals: what would shock you? A 45-60s short holds 3-4 loops; long form holds 8-10 or more.
  3. Work backwards: what context must come first so each reveal lands and is believable?
  4. Order the loops broad to narrow, with dependencies first.
- **Example:** his Zuru home-design short (about 1M views per platform) ran four loops: it feels like a video game, it's built on Fortnite's engine, a 5-year-old could design a real house, and it's 75% cheaper.

### 1.3 The Dopamine Ladder: 6 levels from scroll to superfan

Levels 1-4 are about the message, levels 5-6 about the messenger. The end goal is a Pavlovian response: dopamine just from seeing your face or name.

1. **Stimulation** (first 1-2 seconds): colour, motion, contrast, brightness. Own a distinct look, or the feed numbs people to it.
2. **Captivation:** a curiosity question pops into their head. Bigger and more relevant to your avatar means stronger.
3. **Anticipation:** they guess the answer, and dopamine peaks just before the reveal. Give clues, then head-fake.
4. **Validation:** close the loop with a non-obvious answer. In education, that's the actual tip.
5. **Affection:** they start to like you. Levers: looks, vibe, smiling, and above all solving their problem.
6. **Revelation:** they realise you'll keep delivering value. Far easier for education than entertainment.

### 1.4 The Dopamine Addiction Loop: casino psychology in 4 steps

His core science claim: dopamine is a prediction chemical. It fires while we guess, not when we win, which is why a slot machine hooks people and a vending machine doesn't.

1. **Stakes.** A character to root for (you or a client), something at risk, and urgency. Stakes don't need to be life or death, just personally relevant.
2. **Big question.** Give just enough specifics for the viewer to make a prediction. A vague teaser like "something crazy happened" gives the brain nothing to predict.
3. **Head fake.** Break their prediction with an answer that surprises but makes sense in hindsight. Plant the clues first, like a magician.
4. **Rehook.** Close one loop and open the next in the same breath, with connectors like "which would have been great, except..." or "and that's when I realised...".

His demo retells a flat prince-and-dragon story with the loop switched on: a plague with a one-week deadline, a dragon that turns out to be a chained prisoner, and the king waiting at the top of the tower.

**Kallaway's Dopamine Addiction Loop (4 steps, the last 3 repeat):**

```
1. Stakes  →  2. Big question  →  3. Head fake  →  4. Rehook
                    ↑                                   │
                    └────────── repeat to the end ──────┘
```

Stakes switch the prediction machine on once; the question, head fake and rehook then cycle for the rest of the story.

### 1.5 The 6 Story Locks: word swaps that hold attention

| Lock | What to do | Example swap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Term branding | Give your concepts names (the labeling effect) | "How to improve your offer" becomes Hormozi's "value equation" |
| Embedded truths | Speak as if it's already true | "If you try this" becomes "When you try this" |
| Thought narration | Say what the viewer is thinking right now | "You're probably thinking this can't work for you" |
| Negative frames | Use loss aversion (avoiding pain motivates about 2x more) | "How to grow on YouTube" becomes "This is killing your YouTube growth" |
| Loop openers | Reset the attention hourglass between sections | "But that's not even the most interesting part" |
| Contrast words | The engine under all five | but, actually, instead, turns out, except, yet |

### 1.6 Hooks: Interrupt Theory and the 6 hook words

A hook triggers an orienting response, the involuntary "wait, what was that?" freeze. It must answer two questions: what is this about, and what do I get?

- **The 4 S's:** Subject (crystal clear), Stakes (the outcome they want), Speed (fewest words; compression, not fast talking), Super clear (only one way to read it).
- **The triple hook:** text, visual and spoken hooks (plus the caption's first line) must all point the same way. Misalignment is the top mistake he sees in business owners.
- **Text hook** (the most important, because eyes hunt for text): a subject repeater plus one of a pain reminder, a dream outcome or a future change; specific numbers; power words; at most 2 lines broken at natural phrases; no punctuation except quotes and brackets; no "and", "but" or "therefore"; on screen for about 3 seconds.
- **Spoken hook** = format + power words. Level 1: copy winning formats. Level 2: mine the power phrases in top hooks and remix them (his "scientifically impossible to skip" becomes "psychologically impossible to resist").
- **Visual hook** (least important): move your body, move the visual, or move text.
- **The 6 hook words:** subject, action, objective (end state), contrast, plus an optional proof word ("again") and time word ("in 90 days"). Skeleton: *If I had to grow from 0 to 100K subs on YouTube again in 90 days, here's how I'd do it.*

### 1.7 The lock-in zone: the 1-3 lines after the hook

The hook takes curiosity from 0 to 1; the lock-in zone takes it from 1 to 10. In those seconds the viewer confirms they weren't baited and runs a rapid trust check on your looks, production quality, relatability and proof.

| Method | Use it when | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tease the proof | You have hard numbers | Claim, then "I ran this once and leads went up 4x" |
| Tease the transformation | No hard proof, but a clear desired outcome | Claim, then name the method and the outcome |
| Rapid contrast | Your premise is a bit boring | Common belief, why it's wrong, the alternative |
| Frame the magic box | You have a nameable method | Claim, then "It's called X, it's only 4 steps" |
| Objection handling | There's an obvious reason to scroll | Claim, then kill the objection ("the irony is...") |
| Bold-claim double-down | The key idea could be missed | Say it, then say it again a different way |

### 1.8 The Illusion of Novelty: making boring topics addictive

Addictive content is relevant, novel and interesting. Five steps:

1. **New reveal + outcome mapping:** a new angle on an old thing, tied to an outcome. His example: the time of day you drink water matters more than the amount.
2. **Contrast framing:** set it against a belief they already hold, like the "8 glasses a day" rule. It must be a true opposite.
3. **Urgency** (optional): use recency bias, never fake deadlines.
4. **Bullseye proof:** the trust ladder runs from someone just like the viewer (best), to "lots of people", to a study (weakest).
5. **Protect the illusion:** don't hedge ("never show the mascot without its head"), and deliver like a gossip whisperer sharing a secret, not a town crier.

His twist at the end: the name "illusion of novelty" is itself invented, old persuasion psychology in new packaging.

### 1.9 Conversion storytelling: stories that sell

1. **Avatar framing:** your ideal customer's pain, their vocabulary level, concrete proof, and a path to more.
2. **Broad to narrow:** context, stakes, strategy, tactics, examples, principles, in that order.
3. **Double aha:** two non-obvious insights early, then an aligned CTA right after the second one.
4. **Emotional transformation script:** aggravate the pain, show how the traditional method failed you, tease a *named* new mechanism, show real results, offer an aligned CTA.
5. **Double zoom-out:** when you catch yourself trusting a creator, ask what exactly they said, showed or did.
6. **Buyer psychology:** de-risk the purchase with client proof, your own proof, or skill demonstrated in the content.

### 1.10 The Power of Suggestion: 3 levels of obsession

Let viewers reach the conclusion themselves; people trust conclusions they draw on their own.

1. **Signal:** be consistently more useful than anyone in your niche (relevant, non-obvious, validated).
2. **Belief:** change what they believe with a contrasting frame, a relatable character and small effort for a big reward (his "0 to 100K on YouTube in 5 months").
3. **Action:** overwhelming numerical proof plus steps broken to the smallest unit, so they act and get a result.

His 5-point checklist for every piece: relevant and relatable, small effort for a big reward, non-obvious, backed by proof, and tactically implementable.

### 1.11 The 7 biggest storytelling mistakes

1. **Using the school story arc.** Online, start at about 70/100 intensity, spike the first conflict within 1-2 minutes, then peak and release every 2-5 minutes.
2. **Jumbling the W's.** Lead with what and why, then who and how (credibility), then when and where (just colour).
3. **No re-buy.** After you solve one pain, introduce the next one.
4. **No villain.** It can be a person, a group, an approach or a belief.
5. **Nothing to root for.** Build common ground so viewers see themselves as the hero.
6. **No atomic shareability.** Shrink the idea until it fits in one sentence (Paul Revere). For complex ideas, say it twice: once to a colleague, once as a metaphor for a child.
7. **Not painting the picture.** Use visuals.

### 1.12 Delivery: speaking like a likable expert

- **Camera anxiety:** shake out your body for 30 seconds, do throwaway warm-up takes, tape a friend's photo by the lens, record line by line, and remind yourself this one video doesn't matter.
- **Rhythm:** short sentences, half-second pauses after key words, downward inflection (no upspeak), and no energy drop at sentence ends. Train with *copy speech*: read a top creator's transcript aloud in sync with their video.
- **The likable expert = authority + affinity:** believe your words (rewrite AI scripts in your own voice), be happy to be there, be proof-heavy, ask "how can I be most useful?", and use admission lines about your own struggles.

### 1.13 His 90-day personal-brand blueprint

- **Choose your game:** culturally famous (mass reach) or category famous (faster from zero, more money per follower). Going narrow to broad is easy; the reverse isn't.
- **Write a target statement:** I help \[who\] with \[what\] in \[how\].
- **Build 4 brand pillars:** a niche bullseye, contrarian beliefs (keep a running "contrarian take vault"), associations and signature objects, and points of uniqueness (topic, depth, avatar, format, visuals).
- **Test content formulas** (topic + format + visual layout): about 50 videos in the first 60 days (3, then 5, then 7 a week). Then double down on formulas that perform, attract your ideal viewer, are fun to make, and make you proud.

## 2. Caleb Ralston: the world builder

Caleb (17+ years building personal brands, including on Team GaryVee and as creative director behind two of the biggest business brands; about 170K YouTube subscribers) optimises for trust, not virality. His storytelling is less about second-by-second retention and more about building a world viewers keep coming back to.

### 2.1 The Brand Journey framework: start from the outcome

Reverse-engineer from the outcome you want back to today:

1. What outcome do I want?
2. What would I need to be known for to get it?
3. What would I have to do to be known for that?
4. What do I need to learn to do those things?

If content doesn't move you toward that outcome faster, he says don't make it. Without a defined outcome, "it didn't work" means nothing.

### 2.2 Pick your body of water, and be honest: expert or student?

- **Ocean, lake, pond, puddle.** Your credibility decides which one you can compete in. His example: ocean = business advice; lake = CPG business; pond = CPG founders under $10M a year; puddle = those founders selling on Shopify. Caleb himself plays in "personal brands for business owners", not "branding".
- **Move up** only when your credibility allows it and you have content-market fit where you are.
- **Expert vs student.** An expert teaches in public from what they've done and keeps a *credibility bank*. A student learns in public as the guinea pig and keeps an *interest bank* (what you're studying, testing, experimenting with). Posing as an expert while you're a student burns trust for good; an honest "day 1 of learning" journey gets strong traction.

### 2.3 The 3 levers to stand out

1. **Contrarian belief (the 80% lever).** Contrarian means you genuinely believe something against the dominant view; controversial means it's designed to trigger people, and big brands avoid you for it. His examples are Patagonia (environment over profit) and Netflix (people don't want to leave the house). Find yours with the **two-column exercise**: on the left, what people in your space say (and what competitors do) that you disagree with; on the right, your view. His is "optimise for trust, not virality". It's working when commenters quote it back to you.
2. **Delivery.** Not performing, but removing whatever stops you being yourself. Calm people should be calm on camera; dry, witty people should be dry and witty. Test your understanding: can you explain the idea to three different people in three different situations? Run "game tape" reviews of your own videos. Usefulness beats charisma.
3. **Wrapping paper.** Packaging (title and thumbnail), hook and format: your only advantage before the click. Build a **wrapping-paper library** by screenshotting packaging that stops you (1of10, YouTube, Instagram save folders), including absurd ideas from other niches. His team saved a dating-video title and later realised it fit a video about attracting dream clients.

### 2.4 The content formula

**(Painful problem + your unique solution) = the gift, multiplied by contextual credibility and a proven wrapper.**

- List 10-15 painful problems of your ideal customer. That list is your idea bank.
- **Contextual credibility** is the proof that matches *this* video (for a media-team video: "built a team from 0 to 18 full-timers"), not your generic bio. Write 2-3 versions per problem.
- **Promise, progress, payoff.** Never promise five lessons and then say "comment to get them"; that breaks trust.

### 2.5 The 7 elements of world building (his core storytelling framework)

Fans of Lord of the Rings or Marvel re-enter the world with context already loaded. Do the same in education, so viewers become citizens of your world who use your terms and see through your lens. Expertise earns trust; the world earns loyalty.

1. **Name your concepts.** Name what you already teach so it passes the 3 R's: easy to remember, repeat and reference.
2. **Redefine terms.** Give vague industry words an operational definition. His: branding is an intentional pairing of relevant things done consistently; brand is the association that results.
3. **Share your weaknesses.** Flaws you can change (camera anxiety), limitations you adapt to (ADHD), and costs your choices forced on you. Real, never contrived.
4. **Characters and settings.** A recurring cast (his content director Trevor; GaryVee's DailyVee crew) and recurring places.
5. **Interest stacking.** Your non-niche interests: his Harleys, metal shows, the Seahawks, trash TV. Each shared interest moves a viewer closer, from acquaintance to friend to best friend.
6. **Lore and legends.** Success and failure stories, each paired with a lesson. Failures both teach and prove your depth. Store them in your credibility bank.
7. **Common enemy.** Your contrarian take reframed as the thing standing between your audience and their goal (his: chasing virality). Never a specific person or company; that makes you a "call-out creator". A belief is yours; an enemy turns it into a movement.

### 2.6 Curiosity has 4 inputs

You can't create curiosity directly; you control four inputs: a **knowledge gap**, **belief the gap will be filled** (credibility), **novelty** (different from baseline) and **relevance**. Demographic call-outs like "if you're 45-65 and overweight" hit only relevance. Call out the *consequence* of the pain instead: "tired of wearing a hat all the time?" beats "tired of being bald?". Their rewrite of a member's weak fitness hook added a client's before/after visual and a low-effort promise (under 2 hours a week).

### 2.7 How he structures a video

- **Intro = the 4 C's:** call-out (it can live in the title and thumbnail), credibility (contextual), compass (the roadmap), and core learning: a real nugget in the first \~60 seconds. Hiding the value until late no longer works.
- **Body:** context, principle, a story about the principle, the tactic, an example done right and/or wrong, then a link to the next video.
- **Retention:** in education, learning is the retention hack, not flashy cuts every few seconds.
- **Waterfall method:** one pillar long-form video, mined for self-contained golden moments (an aha, a banger line, a belief-breaker), each repackaged natively for each platform.

### 2.8 Your first 3 videos

1. **Introduction:** why you're starting now, then 3-5 pivotal moments each paired with one usable lesson, then what to expect and a hint of your contrarian belief. Not a brag reel.
2. **Positioning deep dive:** the subject you want to be known for, taught through your contrarian belief, at least 25 minutes. His was a free 6h22m course: 1.1M views, and its workbook alone brought 45K+ leads.
3. **Experiment:** a wildly different format anchored to one useful idea (his: fully scripted lines delivered on a motorcycle ride through Las Vegas). Keep formats you enjoy at low frequency even if the audience is lukewarm.

### 2.9 The ratios and rules he lives by

- **75 / 20 / 5:** deep content only your ideal customer needs, niche-wide content that also helps adjacent people, and personal content sprinkled through everything (steak, sides, dessert). No traction after \~6 months? Fix positioning and hooks first, then open niche-wide by 5-10%.
- **70 / 20 / 10:** proven formats, iterations that change one variable, and big experiments, so you find the next format before your audience gets tired of the current one.
- **Case studies:** he says almost all the content he has made for clients is "we did this, we got that".
- **Don't-do list:** what you won't do or be associated with (he won't put famous clients' faces on thumbnails, even though it would boost clicks).
- **Humanity is the one thing AI can't copy.** Show it through where you film, the words you stop cutting and the references you make.
- **Expect nothing meaningful for 36 months.** He got 100K subscribers in 18 months posting about once a month, and 265K followers in year one, but treats that as a bonus, not the plan.

## 3. Sam Gaudet: the operator

Sam (25; creative director behind Dan Martell's brand, which he says grew from under 100K to 12M+ followers; his own brand went from about zero to 75K followers in nine months, shot mostly on an iPhone) is the most tactical of the three. He gives you frameworks you can script tonight and a system you can run in a few hours a week.

### 3.1 The storytelling equation and his 5 principles

1. **Context + conflict = curiosity.** Tension makes people lean in. His example is Matthew McConaughey describing feeling boxed in by his rom-com era just as his family life was changing.
2. **Only essential context:** where were you, when was it, what were you doing? Then go straight to the conflict. Template: *In \[year\], I was \[place or activity\] when \[person\] \[conflict\].* His own: in 2023, at the gym, Dan Martell called about building a media company.
3. **Share thoughts and emotions.** Ask "what was I thinking?" and "what was I feeling?" Facts alone sound robotic; feelings create connection.
4. **Tone and delivery,** borrowed from Obama's 2008 victory speech: say "you", not "you guys"; end sentences low instead of with upspeak; slow down and embrace the pauses.
5. **Get on more stages:** posts, podcasts, even a wedding toast. Reps are the only gap between an okay storyteller and a world-class one.

### 3.2 HEIT: his short-form framework (the "yap" method)

| Step | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **H**ook | Context + a contrarian take + an open loop | His "Trial reels aren't dead, they're just misunderstood" (200K+ views) |
| **E**xplain | Why it matters to the viewer | How the tactic changed their results |
| **I**llustrate | A story, example or analogy linking the unknown to the known | Content without a plan is like a puzzle without the picture on the box |
| **T**each | One simple idea they can apply today | One idea, not a 7-step list |

- **Anatomy of a yap = topic + angle + format.** Same topic, flipped angle, different result: Dan Martell's "meetings are the biggest time waster" got 2.3M views; "meetings are great" wouldn't have.
- **Pick topics by demand, fit and interest.** Fit uses Paddy Galloway's CCN idea: the topic should work for your core, casual and new audiences. Interest means you genuinely care; the audience can tell when you don't.
- **His pyramid of importance:** topic at the top, then delivery and tone, then hooks and formats, with gear and editing at the bottom.
- **Extras:** 1-2 lines of on-screen text (5-10 words), test different environments (car, office, home), keep delivery calm rather than imitating MrBeast, and use graphics as visual hooks.

### 3.3 Long form: Triple P, sections and asymmetric pacing

- **Intro = Triple P:** Promise (what you'll learn), Proof (why you're qualified), Plan (the roadmap, shown on screen).
- **Each section:** Point, Explain, Illustrate, Lesson.
- **Asymmetric pacing:** make the first point the shortest (about 30s), then about 1, 1.5 and 2 minutes. It creates an illusion of progress, like MrBeast's fast openings or ad progress bars that start fast.
- **Progressive density:** open with a broad problem everyone feels, then get more tactical toward the end as a wink to your core audience. Dan's meetings video ended with scorecards, KPIs and owners; by October Sam cited 4M views and 10K net new followers for it.
- **Scripts are guide-rails, not words to read.** Tangents feel authentic. Write by hand; use AI to generate many angles (he suggests 50) and pick one.
- **"Your content isn't too long, it's too slow."** Pack the most value into the least time.

### 3.4 Making ideas worth hearing

- **The 4 N's filter:** net new, non-obvious, nuanced, observable.
- **Often it's just framing.** Dan Martell didn't teach "how to delegate"; he named it the Buyback principle (buy back your time).
- **Say old things to new people.** Repackage fundamental truths instead of chasing brand-new ideas.
- **Model, then modify.** Validate demand first (what works for others, your past posts, Reddit, X), because joining an existing conversation is easier than starting one. Then make it yours with your own stories.
- **If everyone agrees with you, you're probably not saying anything.** Pick the hill you'll die on. His: topic and idea matter more than gear, lighting and editing.

### 3.5 Building a cult-like brand: his 12 principles

1. **People follow characters.** His *character diamond*: north star (what you want to be known for), kryptonite (a real weakness), non-negotiables (hills you'll die on) and weird interests.
2. **Brand is association, not a logo.** Nike's swoosh meant little until Michael Jordan wore it.
3. **Cults have their own language.** Build "handle frameworks" people can repeat (Dan Martell's 10-80-10 rule; Sam's HEIT).
4. **Self-articulation.** Say what viewers feel but can't put into words. Lead with what they actually want (more money), not your mechanism (a bigger team).
5. **Common enemy.** His is "you don't need expensive gear". Dan's "Boss vs Leader" carousel got 10M+ impressions as people roasted their bosses. A bigger enemy makes a bigger hero.
6. **Losses build more trust than wins.** He publicly admitted missing his goal of 100K followers in 6 months, and people rallied behind him.
7. **Give your best stuff away free;** sell the implementation.
8. **Abundance of content, scarcity of access.**
9. **Recognition is the cheapest loyalty:** reply, reshare, say thank you.
10. **Show, don't tell.**
11. **Do cool things, then talk about them.** He films about 3 hours a week; the rest is real work that feeds his stories.
12. **People share content to label themselves.** Make content people want to be seen sharing.

### 3.6 Personal brand from zero

- **Known-for vs paid-for matrix:** aim for the top-right corner, known *and* paid for the right thing. Ask: in 20 years, what do I want to be known and paid for?
- **3-5 contrarian takes,** repeated constantly.
- **Post less, focus more:** one primary platform for 6-12 months. His pick from scratch: Instagram first, then YouTube.
- **Proof stacking:** publicly chase your next result (his: 100K followers with only an iPhone) so new proof stacks on old.
- **Movement marketing:** share your mission. Dan Martell's is helping entrepreneurs build a business they don't grow to hate.
- **Shared language:** coin phrases and repeat them for years (Dan's "exhaust the body, tame the mind"; Greg LaVecchia's "moonshot").
- **Create a customer, don't just capture one.** Free content can take someone from unaware to buyer: his friend went from broke on Sam's couch at 19 to buying Hormozi's $5K, $35K and $135K programs.
- **Don't monetise for about 6 months.** Give until people ask you to sell.
- **Content is a web, not a funnel:** move your earned audience (Instagram, YouTube, TikTok) to owned channels (email, SMS).
- **Document everything from day one.**

### 3.7 His system: big growth on little time

- **Lake method instead of waterfall:** make native content for 1-2 primary platforms, then repost it to secondary ones.
- **Weekly loop:** ideate, create, produce, distribute, optimise. Re-shoot 10x outliers with a new angle or format; repost winners older than 60 days (play your greatest hits).
- **Block the time.** His week is about 1h ideating, 20 min shooting and 2h editing for shorts, plus 1h ideating and 30 min shooting for long form.
- **Elon Musk's 5-step algorithm on your process:** question requirements, delete, simplify, speed up the cycle, automate last.
- **Volume negates luck.**
- **A caution on distribution:** his trial-reels tactics include posting the same file as a trial reel and, 24 hours later, to your main feed. He also teaches altering old videos to dodge Instagram's duplicate-content detection, which carries platform-policy risk.

## 4. Where they disagree, and how to resolve it

They agree on principles and split on execution, mostly because each optimises for a different goal: Kallaway for attention, Caleb for trust with high-ticket buyers, Sam for fast growth on little time. Use the last column as the default and test it.

## 5. The master story template

This 10-beat template fuses the three: Sam's HEIT as the spine, Kallaway's loops and locks as the engine, and Caleb's credibility and humanity as the trust layer. It fits a 45-60 second short; the long-form version follows.

### Short form (45-60 seconds)

1. **Hook (0-3s).** Subject + stake + contrast in under 10 words, with a text hook of at most 2 lines and something moving on screen. Best shape: a contrarian take that opens a loop. *(Kallaway's 6 words, Sam's HEIT hook)*
2. **Lock-in zone (3-10s).** One of the six: tease the proof, tease the transformation, rapid contrast, a named method, kill the objection, or double down. *(Kallaway)*
3. **Stakes and essential context.** Where, when, what you were doing, and what was at risk. Nothing else. *(Sam, Kallaway)*
4. **The big question.** Enough specifics that the viewer can guess what happens. *(Kallaway)*
5. **First loop with feeling.** Context, then "but" or "therefore", then a reveal. Say what you thought and felt. *(Kallaway, Sam)*
6. **Head fake.** The answer they didn't predict, with the clues already planted. *(Kallaway)*
7. **Rehook.** Close that loop and open the next in one sentence ("which would have worked, except..."). *(Kallaway)*
8. **Illustrate.** A story, example or analogy that links your idea to something they already know. *(Sam)*
9. **Teach one thing, and name it.** One implementable idea with a memorable name. *(Sam, Caleb, Kallaway)*
10. **Last dab.** A line worth repeating that echoes the hook so the video loops, or an aligned CTA. *(Kallaway)*

### Fill-in script (swap every bracket for something real)

```markdown
[Contrarian hook: "Most people think X. That's exactly why Y."]
[Lock-in: "I tested this on __ and got __." or "It's called the __ method."]
In [month], I was [doing what] when [what went wrong].
I remember thinking [thought]. Honestly, I felt [emotion].
So I tried [obvious fix], but [why it failed].
Turns out the problem wasn't [what everyone assumes]. It was [the real cause].
Which would have been fine, except [new complication].
It's like [analogy from everyday life].
So here's the one thing: [the idea]. I call it [name].
[Last line that echoes the hook.]
```

### Long form (10-25 minutes)

- **Intro:** call-out, contextual credibility, the plan on screen, and one real nugget in the first 60 seconds (Caleb's 4 C's plus Sam's Triple P).
- **Sections:** point, explain, a story that illustrates it, the lesson. Make section 1 the shortest and let each next one run longer (asymmetric pacing).
- **Between sections:** a loop opener every 60-90 seconds, and a new peak every 2-5 minutes.
- **By minute 8-10:** land your second aha, then an aligned CTA.
- **Throughout:** at least one failure story (lore), one recurring character or setting, and one interest from outside your niche.
- **Close:** leave them with your point of view as their new lens, and link the next video.
